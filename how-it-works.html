How it works

Everything on this site is derived from broadcast video, so the only thing that makes a number worth reading is being able to see where it came from. This page is that explanation: what gets measured, who checks it, what each label means, and what we refuse to claim.

What “verified” means here

A composition is one team's five heroes at one moment on one map. The tracker never asks a person to type that in from memory, and never takes it from a scoreboard site. It reads the ten hero portraits in the broadcast HUD directly, by matching each portrait against real hero crops harvested from the same broadcast.

A composition is published only if it is in one of two states:

  • Human reviewed — a person looked at the crops the detector used and confirmed the read (or corrected it; a correction always beats the machine, and the original machine read is kept alongside it rather than deleted).
  • Auto-high — the detector's match was strong and stable enough across consecutive samples to clear the high-confidence gate on its own.

Anything else — a low-confidence read, a slot the detector could not identify, a run that is still in progress — is not shown at all. It is not shown greyed out, or with an asterisk. It stays out of the public dataset and out of every average computed from it. That is the single rule the whole site is built around.

The five stages

Two of these stages are a human saying yes. That is deliberate: the expensive failure mode for a tracker is not being slow, it is being confidently wrong.

  1. 01

    Register the broadcast

    A VOD is added — pasted in as a link, or found automatically on the official OWCS channels using their public feeds (no API key, no quota). Registering a link imports metadata only; nothing is downloaded or trusted yet.

  2. 02

    Calibrate the HUD

    Every broadcast lays its hero portraits out slightly differently. The calibrator computes where the ten portrait boxes actually sit, verifies the fit against real pixels, and refuses to continue below its confidence threshold instead of guessing a layout. A human approves the resulting layout profile.

  3. 03

    Read the map

    Frames are sampled across the map. Each frame is first classified as live gameplay or not — replays, killcams, scoreboards and desk segments are thrown away, because a replay would otherwise create a composition that never happened. Every surviving portrait slot is template-matched, and the detector records its best candidate, the runner-up and the margin between them. Too close to call returns UNKNOWN.

  4. 04

    Human review

    Proposed identities (which teams, which map, which players) and the detections themselves pass a human gate. Approvals are audited: they record who approved what and when. Nothing on this site was approved by the machine that produced it.

  5. 05

    Publish with receipts

    Approved compositions are exported to the public dataset together with the run, frames and crops behind them. If any link of that chain is missing, the export drops the composition rather than shipping an unprovable one.

The evidence chain

Every published composition can be walked backwards, and the site links each hop:

composition → capture run → sampled frames → hero crops → review status

On any match page, the Evidence tab shows that chain for real: the capture window, the resolution actually captured (flagged if it differs from what was requested), the frames that were sampled, and the exact HUD crops the detector compared. The Review tab shows what is still held back and what commands would move it forward.

If you ever see a number on this site you can't trace to frames, that is a bug worth reporting — not a feature.

Swaps and the rejection ledger

A mid-map hero swap is the hardest thing to read off a broadcast, because a dead player's dimmed portrait, a killcam or a single noisy frame can all look like a switch. The tracker treats a swap as a temporal event: a slot must hold its new hero across several consecutive samples, with enough margin, before the change is confirmed.

Candidates that fail that test are not silently dropped — they are recorded with the reason they were thrown out, and shown publicly on the swap intelligence page. A tracker that only publishes its hits is impossible to audit; the rejection ledger is how you can tell the difference between “nothing happened” and “we didn't look”.

How the stats are counted

  • The unit is one team on one map. A hero played by a team for a whole map counts once, no matter how many frames it appeared in — so a long map can't inflate a pick rate.
  • Pick rate = the appearances of that hero ÷ all team-map appearances in the current filter. Win rate = wins ÷ decided maps; a map with no recorded winner counts as a pick and not as a loss.
  • Win rate is blank, not zero, when nothing has been decided yet. Zero is a claim; blank is the truth.
  • Bans are match facts, imported from official sources and always labelled as such. They are never inferred from video and never mixed into pick rates.
  • Withheld matches contribute nothing. If a match is held back from publication, it is excluded from every aggregate on the site, not just from its own page.

What we don't claim

  • Coverage is small. This is a young project with a handful of fully processed maps. Rates derived from them are honest arithmetic over a small sample — read them as “what happened in these maps”, not “the state of the meta”.
  • Series scores are not invented. Where a scoreline was never imported as a fact, the site shows “–”.
  • Timing is sample-accurate, not frame-accurate. Round boundaries and swap timestamps come from sampling every few seconds and can be off by about one sample.
  • Match facts and compositions never cross over. Schedules, teams and scores come from official or manually entered facts; compositions come only from reviewed capture. Neither is ever used to fill a gap in the other.
  • Rosters can be empty. A team with no confirmed line-up shows nothing rather than plausible-looking names.
  • Art and marks. Hero portraits shown as evidence are broadcast crops. Teams without a verified official mark get a designed crest, never a guessed logo.

Run it yourself

The whole pipeline is plain Python and OpenCV with no paid services, and the site is static HTML that any host can serve. From a checkout you can paste a broadcast link into the operator portal and drive every stage from the browser, or run the same commands directly:

python pipeline/automation/cli.py convert-link --url "<youtube-url>"

On a hosted copy like this one there is no server behind the page, so the portal turns read-only and builds the exact command for you instead of pretending to work. That is also why the portal is a separate page from the site you're reading: nothing in the results needs it.
